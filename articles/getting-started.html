Measure your kitchen walls against your cabinet drawings, and take time to find the highest spot on the finished floor using a long level. Measure up from that high spot to mark the reference line for the top of your base cabinets and the bottom of your upper cabinets. Having your level lines clearly marked on the drywall prevents wavy sightlines later on.

Use a stud finder to identify every wall stud along the layout, marking stud centers with a pencil so you know exactly where cabinet screws must bite. Make sure you know where electrical wires, water pipes, or gas lines run behind the drywall before you run fasteners into the wall. Shut off power and water to the room before work gets underway to avoid dangerous accidents.

Remove all doors, drawers, and shelving from the cabinet boxes so the units are light and easy to maneuver. Fasten a temporary wooden ledger board along your upper reference line to support the weight of the wall cabinets while you work. Begin with your corner wall unit, shim it plumb and level, and drive screws through the cabinet hanging strips directly into the studs.

Slide the next wall cabinet against the first, clamping their face frames flush before drilling pilot holes and screwing the frames together. Once joined, drive mounting screws through the cabinet back into the wall studs, checking alignment continuously across the run. Never rely on drywall anchors alone, because loaded wall cabinets require full structural support.

Position your corner or end base cabinet at the highest point of the floor and use shims underneath to bring it perfectly level. Fasten adjacent base units together through their face frames, secure the boxes into the wall studs, and check that the front edge stays straight. Once the boxes are secured, take down the upper ledger board, patch any screw holes, and reinstall your doors, drawers, and hardware.

Repainting a kitchen is much more demanding than refreshing an ordinary bedroom. The unique combination of food oils, boiling steam, and high foot traffic leads to specific painting mistakes that cause finishes to fail early.

  • Painting directly over cooking grease

Cooking oils settle invisibly on kitchen walls, especially around the range and vent hood. Applying new paint directly over this residue prevents proper bonding, causing the paint to blister, crawl, or peel off in sheets within months. Always wash surfaces with a proper degreasing solution first.

  • Skipping primer over drywall repairs

Applying topcoat directly over patched holes creates noticeable dull patches known as flashing. Drywall compound absorbs paint differently than surrounding painted walls. A stain-blocking primer seals the repair and provides a consistent base for uniform sheen.

  • Using flat paint in high-moisture zones

Flat or matte sheens absorb moisture and stains easily, making them almost impossible to wipe clean without burnishing the surface. High-traffic kitchens benefit from durable eggshell, satin, or semi-gloss sheens designed for regular washing and humidity resistance.

  • Rushing the recoat and cure times

Applying a second coat before the first has dried at least four to six hours traps moisture beneath the surface film. While paint may feel dry to the touch quickly, full curing takes days, meaning heavy cooking with steam should be delayed.

If your kitchen walls show multiple layers of peeling paint, grease saturation, or damaged drywall, a full professional prep and paint job delivers lasting durability. Even expensive custom cabinets will look amateurish if the surrounding trim is poorly fitted. Homeowners frequently run into aesthetic and functional headaches when decorative molding is rushed. Here are five common kitchen trim mistakes and how to avoid them during your remodel.

Measuring only one point on an uneven wall

Drywall is rarely straight. Cutting filler strips to a single measurement leaves noticeable gaps at the top or bottom of the cabinet. Always measure at multiple heights, cut your filler slightly wider than the largest gap, and scribe the exact wall contour for a tight, custom fit.

Nailing crown molding directly into empty cabinet tops

Shooting finish nails straight down into thin cabinet box frames often splits the wood or fails to anchor the molding securely. Install solid wood sub-fascia or nailer blocks above the cabinet boxes first to provide a solid, concealed nailing surface.

Relying on caulk to conceal large fitting gaps

A heavy bead of caulk along out-of-square crown miters or wavy drywall seams will eventually shrink, yellow, or crack. Caulk should only act as a hairline bridge on painted woodwork, never as a structural patch for bad cuts or missing scribes.

Overlooking light-rail clearance for under-cabinet lights

Installing under-cabinet trim without measuring your light fixtures can leave wires or pucks visible from adjoining dining chairs. Measure fixture thickness first so the light rail fully conceals your hardware.

Forgetting door swing clearance at fillers

Placing decorative trim too close to adjacent walls can block cabinet doors from opening past 90 degrees, trapping roll-out trays inside. Always maintain adequate clearance between hinges and perpendicular walls.

Adding decorative molding transforms plain kitchen cabinets, but small installation errors quickly become obvious eyesores once the tools are put away.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Measuring only once: Wall runs and cabinet faces shift slightly from corner to corner. Always double-check measurements at both top and bottom edges before making your first cut.
  • Guessing the spring angle: Cabinet crown molding commonly rests at either 38 or 45 degrees. Cutting with the wrong angle setting produces corners that will never sit flush.

  • Using caulk as a fix for wide gaps: Caulk handles tiny hairline joints, but using it to bridge major discrepancies looks messy and shrinks over time. Substantial ceiling dips require scribing or trim fillers instead.
