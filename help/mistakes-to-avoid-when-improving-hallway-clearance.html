Mistakes to Avoid When Improving Hallway Clearance

Home About Services Reviews Contact FAQs Articles Team Members Web Pages

Making your hallways feel more open and functional is a great goal, but it's easy to fall into some common traps. Knowing what to watch out for can save you time, money, and frustration down the line. Here are a few mistakes we often see and how to steer clear of them:

  • Measuring Wall-to-Wall Only: This is probably the biggest oversight! You might think your hallway is 40 inches wide, but if you're not accounting for door trim, baseboards, or even light switches, your actual clear width for passing could be significantly less. Always measure the narrowest point from finished surface to finished surface, factoring in any projections.
  • Forgetting About Door Swings: A door that opens directly into a hallway can drastically reduce your usable clear width when it's open. Forgetting to consider how a door's swing impacts circulation is a common error. Think about changing the swing direction or even exploring pocket or barn doors if feasible.
  • Adding Obstructions Post-Remodel: You've just worked to widen your hallway, and then you add a console table or a bulky piece of art. It sounds obvious, but it happens! Be mindful of how new furniture or decor might reduce the path you just worked hard to create. Aim for flush or recessed storage if you need it.
  • Ignoring Accessibility Standards (Even if Not Required): While you might not be legally required to meet ADA standards in your home, aiming for more generous clearances (like 44-48 inches for comfortable two-way passage, or 60 inches for wheelchair passing) makes your home more comfortable and future-proof. Don't just aim for the bare minimum without considering real-world use.
  • Taking on Structural Changes Alone: If widening a hallway involves moving or altering framing, especially load-bearing walls, this is definitely not a DIY project. Doing it incorrectly can compromise your home's structure.
